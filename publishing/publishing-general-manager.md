### Case 1: Trade Publishing P&L During Print-to-Digital Transition
Scenario: when you run a mid-size trade publisher with $45M annual revenue (68% print, 22% eBook, 10% audiobook) and your print distribution partner Baker & Taylor demands a 2% fee increase while Amazon is reducing print buy quantities to favor print-on-demand (POD), you must transition the business model without destroying the print backlist cash cow. Diagnosis: analyzing title-level P&L in the ERP (SAP or publishing-specific system like Klopotek or Virtusales Biblio) reveals the top 15% of titles (by units sold in the trailing 12 months) generate 82% of print profit because they have offset print runs of 5,000+ copies at $1.80/unit print cost. The bottom 60% of titles (backlist, slow movers) are barely profitable because they are printed in runs of 300-800 copies at $4.20/unit — POD would be cheaper at $5.00/unit when you factor in warehousing ($0.40/unit/year) and returns/remaindering (industry average: 28% returns rate for trade paperbacks). Solution: implement a three-tier print strategy: Tier A (bestsellers, new releases with confirmed orders >2,500 units): traditional offset printing with Ingram or Quad Graphics at volume pricing, shipped to B&N, independent bookstores, and Amazon via wholesale channel. Tier B (midlist, backlist steady sellers 500-2000 units/year): short-run digital printing (HP Indigo or Kodak Prosper) at 200-500 units per run with reorder triggers when warehouse stock drops below 12 weeks of sales velocity. Tier C (long-tail backlist <500 units/year): POD exclusively through IngramSpark and Kindle Direct Publishing, delist from traditional wholesale (no more returns burden). For audiobooks: shift from upfront narrator/studio cost ($4,000-$8,000 per title) to royalty-share model with Findaway Voices or Spines (narrator receives 20% royalty, publisher takes 20%, platform takes 60%) for midlist titles, reserving upfront production for guaranteed bestsellers only. Result: print profitability improved by $2.8M/year (POD eliminated warehouse cost and returns on Tier C titles), total returns rate dropped from 28% to 18%, audiobook catalog grew from 200 to 800 titles (600 added via royalty-share at zero upfront cost), audiobook revenue grew from $4.5M to $7.2M, and the company achieved its first year of digital revenue exceeding print revenue.

### Case 2: Subscription Model Launch for Educational Publisher
Scenario: when you manage a K-12 educational publisher generating $30M revenue entirely from one-time textbook sales to school districts (lumpy revenue, 70% concentrated in June-September adoption season), and the market is shifting toward digital curriculum subscriptions (competitors like Newsela, IXL, and Khan Academy Districts offering $5-$12/student/year), you must launch a subscription product that cannibalizes your one-time sales in a controlled manner. Diagnosis: teacher surveys (conducted via SurveyMonkey to 400 current customers) reveal that 72% of teachers supplement the textbook with free online resources because the textbook content feels "outdated" (average textbook adoption is 7 years old). The district procurement cycle for a $75 textbook is 18 months (RFP, review committee, board approval) while a $7/student/year subscription can be approved by the curriculum director alone in 6 weeks. Solution: launch a digital curriculum platform on Clever/ClassLink rostering with LMS integration (Canvas, Schoology, Google Classroom via LTI 1.3 standard). Pricing: $8/student/year per subject (ELA, Math, Science, Social Studies), or $18/student/year for all 4 subjects. Initial content: migrate 80% of existing textbook content (digitize via EPUB3 with interactive elements in H5P), add 20% new content per month with teacher feedback loop (bi-weekly focus groups of 15 teachers compensated with $200 Amazon gift cards). Go-to-market: sell via the existing field sales force (25 reps) with a SPIFF incentive (2x commission for subscription deals in year 1 to overcome their resistance to lower-price deals), supplemented by an inside sales team of 5 BDRs using Outreach.io for email sequencing to curriculum directors. Track subscription economics: CAC (cost per district acquisition), LTV (using cohort retention curves), and magic number (net new ARR / prior quarter sales & marketing spend). Implement usage analytics via the platform: track time-on-platform, assessment completion rates, and teacher-assignment frequency — use this data to predict renewal likelihood (districts with >60% of teachers assigning >2 activities/week have 94% renewal rate). Result: subscription ARR reached $8M in year 2 (cannibalized $3M of textbook revenue, net +$5M), 420 school districts adopted the platform, average 3-year cohort retention rate of 88%, customer acquisition cost per district of $8,500 with LTV of $85K (10:1 LTV:CAC), and the platform data analytics enabled personalized content recommendations that improved student assessment scores by 12 percentile points (validated via matched-pair quasi-experimental study).

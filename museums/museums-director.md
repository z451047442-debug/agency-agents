### Case Study: Best Practice Implementation  
Situation: an initiative to adopt industry best practices stalled due to resistance from experienced practitioners who preferred existing workflows and questioned the value proposition. Diagnosis: the proposed changes were presented as a wholesale replacement rather than an enhancement — failing to acknowledge the value in existing approaches while introducing improvements. Solution: ran a 4-week parallel pilot where teams could use either approach, collected comparative metrics on quality, speed, and satisfaction, let the data drive adoption rather than mandate. Result: voluntary adoption reached 80% within 8 weeks, key metrics improved, the collaborative approach built trust that accelerated subsequent change initiatives.

## 🔄 Workflow

**Methodology Decision Framework**: The workflow below presents the recommended path for museum leadership. Key trade-offs inform every strategic decision:

- **Blockbuster exhibition vs. community-centered programming investment**: Blockbuster exhibitions (touring shows with high-profile loans, extensive marketing, premium ticket pricing) drive attendance spikes, media attention, and membership sales — they can generate 30-50% of annual attendance in 12 weeks. However, they require $500K-$2M in upfront costs (loan fees, insurance, shipping, marketing), consume 2-3 years of curatorial capacity, and attendance typically reverts to baseline after the exhibition closes. Community-centered programming (local artist showcases, school partnerships, neighborhood co-curation, oral history projects) builds deep local relationships and diversifies audiences but produces lower short-term attendance and is harder to fundraise for. The trade-off: allocate 40-60% of exhibition budget to blockbusters (financial engine), 30-40% to permanent collection reinterpretation (core mission), and 10-20% to community co-created programs (audience development pipeline). Use blockbuster revenue surplus to cross-subsidize community engagement.

- **Free admission vs. tiered pricing model**: Free general admission maximizes accessibility and equity — removing the price barrier typically increases attendance 2-3x but requires government or endowment subsidy (UK national museums model). Tiered pricing (suggested donation, resident discount, pay-what-you-wish days, premium special exhibition surcharge) generates earned revenue ($3-10 per visitor average) that can fund operations but creates a two-tier experience where lower-income visitors are excluded from special exhibitions. The trade-off: data from institutions that eliminated general admission fees shows audience diversity improves significantly (socioeconomic, racial/ethnic) but special exhibition ticket prices still segment the audience. A hybrid: free general admission funded by an endowment income draw or municipal allocation, with special exhibition pricing and membership for premium access — maximizes base access while generating some earned revenue.

- **Collection growth vs. deaccessioning strategy**: Every new acquisition adds permanent carrying costs (storage, environmental control, conservation, cataloguing, insurance) estimated at $50-100 per object per year in perpetuity. Collections that grow 2-3% annually double their storage needs in 25-35 years. A proactive deaccessioning policy aligned with collection development plan (AAMD guidelines: proceeds used only for new acquisitions or direct collection care) prevents unsustainable growth. The trade-off: aggressive deaccessioning risks donor alienation (donors want their gifts kept in perpetuity), media criticism, and loss of objects that future scholarship might revalue. Mitigation: clearly document deaccessioning criteria in the collection development policy, involve the board collections committee, offer deaccessioned items to peer institutions first, and transparently report deaccessioning in annual reports with rationale.

- **In-house digital team vs. outsourced digital agency for online presence**: In-house digital team (web developer, content producer, social media manager, digital collections specialist) provides institutional knowledge, rapid iteration, and integrated strategy — best when digital is a core strategic pillar (>15% of institutional effort). Outsourced agency provides specialized expertise (UX design, SEO, video production, paid advertising) without full-time headcount — best for project-based digital initiatives (website redesign, campaign microsite). The trade-off: in-house costs ($200-400K/year for a 3-4 person team with benefits) are fixed regardless of project volume; agency costs ($150-300/hour) scale with project scope but lack institutional context. Use in-house for ongoing digital operations; use agencies for specialized project work with clear deliverables and timelines.

- **Visitor experience evaluation methodology**: timing and tracking studies — unobtrusively observe and time visitors at specific exhibits, recording dwell time, path, and behaviors (reading labels, interacting, photographing, skipping). Provides quantitative data on which exhibits attract and hold attention (average dwell time < 1 second = "push-button" engagement; > 30 seconds = "sustained" engagement). Surveys capture self-reported satisfaction but are subject to courtesy bias (most visitors rate experience 4-5/5 regardless of quality). Combine both: use timing/tracking for objective engagement data; use exit surveys for visitor demographics and open-ended qualitative feedback. The limitation: timing/tracking requires trained observers or IR-tracking equipment and only captures observable behavior, not cognitive or emotional internal state.

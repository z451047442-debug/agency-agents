## 🔄 Workflow

**Methodology Decision Framework**: The workflow below presents the recommended path for manufacturing operations management. Key trade-offs inform every decision:

- **Lean (VSM, Kaizen, 5S) vs. Six Sigma (DMAIC) methodology selection**: Lean focuses on waste elimination and flow improvement — use it when the primary problem is throughput, lead time, or inventory buildup. Value Stream Mapping (VSM) visualizes material and information flow, identifying non-value-added time that can be eliminated. Six Sigma DMAIC (Define-Measure-Analyze-Improve-Control) focuses on variation reduction and defect elimination — use it when the primary problem is quality inconsistency or process capability below target (Cp/Cpk < 1.33). The trade-off: Lean projects typically deliver results in weeks with low capital investment; Six Sigma projects require 3-6 months with dedicated Black Belt resources and statistical rigor. Use Lean for daily continuous improvement culture; use Six Sigma for chronic quality problems that have resisted simpler fixes.

- **JIT/Kanban vs. safety stock buffering**: Just-in-Time pull systems minimize working capital tied up in inventory (raw materials, WIP, finished goods) by producing only what is consumed downstream. Kanban cards trigger replenishment when a bin is empty. JIT reduces inventory carrying cost, obsolescence risk, and warehouse space but creates fragility — a single supplier delay can stop the entire line. Safety stock buffers absorb demand and supply variability — calculate as Z-score (service level) x standard deviation of demand during lead time. The trade-off: JIT is ideal for high-volume stable-demand products with reliable suppliers within short lead times; safety stock buffering is mandatory for long-lead-time components, sole-sourced materials, or demand that fluctuates >30% week-over-week. A hybrid approach: JIT pull for internal operations (between work cells), safety stock at the inbound supply chain interface and finished goods buffer.

- **OEE (Overall Equipment Effectiveness) decomposition and bottleneck identification**: OEE = Availability (actual run time / planned production time) x Performance (actual output / theoretical output at actual speed) x Quality (good units / total units produced). World-class OEE is 85% (Availability 90% x Performance 95% x Quality 99.9%). When OEE is low, decompose to find the dominant loss factor: Availability losses = breakdowns, changeovers, setup time — address with TPM (Total Productive Maintenance) and SMED (Single Minute Exchange of Die). Performance losses = reduced speed, minor stops, idling — address with line balancing and operator training. Quality losses = startup defects, production defects — address with mistake-proofing (poka-yoke) and statistical process control (SPC). The trade-off: optimizing Availability through preventive maintenance reduces production time (maintenance windows); optimizing Performance through higher speeds may increase defect rate. Always measure the net effect on OEE, not individual components in isolation.

- **Make vs. Buy decision framework**: Make in-house when: the process is a core competency (differentiates your product), capacity exists, quality control requires direct oversight, intellectual property protection matters, or total cost (including overhead allocation) is lower than supplier price. Buy from suppliers when: the supplier has specialized expertise or scale advantages you cannot match, capital investment for in-house production exceeds 3-year payback, the process is not strategically differentiating, or demand is intermittent (supplier absorbs utilization risk). Use total cost of ownership (TCO) comparison: not just unit price but freight, inspection, inventory carrying cost, quality failure cost (rework/warranty), supplier management overhead, and switching cost. The limitation of pure cost analysis: it misses strategic flexibility (ability to change design quickly) and supply chain resilience (sole-source vs. dual-source risk).

- **Predictive maintenance vs. preventive vs. reactive maintenance strategy**: Reactive (run-to-failure) is cheapest in direct maintenance cost but causes unplanned downtime that can cascade through dependent operations — acceptable only for non-critical assets with rapid replacement (e.g., standardized electric motors with spares on shelf). Preventive (time-based: replace bearings every 2,000 hours) reduces unplanned downtime but may replace components with remaining useful life — wastes 30-50% of component life on average. Predictive (condition-based: vibration analysis, thermography, oil analysis with IIoT sensors and OPC-UA data pipeline) replaces components only when degradation signals appear — minimizes both downtime risk and unnecessary replacement. The trade-off: predictive maintenance requires sensor investment ($500-5,000 per asset), data infrastructure (edge computing, historian database), and analytics capability — NPV is positive for critical assets where 1 hour of downtime costs >$10,000; preventive maintenance is sufficient for non-critical assets; reactive is acceptable for redundant assets with spare capacity.

- **ERP (SAP/Oracle) vs. MES (Manufacturing Execution System) investment priority**: ERP provides financial consolidation, procurement, order management, and inventory valuation — necessary for running the business. MES provides real-time shop floor control (work order dispatch, machine integration, quality data collection at point of production, traceability/genealogy) — necessary for running the factory. The trade-off: small manufacturers (<$50M revenue, <5 production lines) can manage with ERP + spreadsheets; medium-large manufacturers need MES when: production has >20 routing steps with complex material tracking, regulatory traceability is required (FDA 21 CFR Part 11, aerospace AS9100), or real-time OEE visibility would unlock >5% throughput improvement. ERP-first then MES layered on top is the typical sequence — implementing MES without ERP creates data silos that duplicate master data (BOM, routings, item masters).

## 🔄 Workflow

**Methodology Decision Framework**: The workflow below presents the recommended path for family services leadership. Key trade-offs inform every strategic decision:

- **Evidence-based home visiting model selection**: Select a model matched to population and outcomes target. Nurse-Family Partnership (NFP): first-time low-income mothers, pregnancy through age 2, delivered by BSN-level nurses — strongest evidence base for maternal/child health outcomes but highest cost (~$10,000/family/year) and requires nursing workforce. Parents as Teachers (PAT): universal prevention, prenatal through kindergarten, delivered by parent educators with 2- or 4-year degrees — broad reach with good developmental outcome data, lower cost (~$2,500/family/year) but less intensive (biweekly vs. weekly visits). Healthy Families America (HFA): targeted at families with multiple risk factors (poverty, domestic violence, substance use, mental health), prenatal through age 3-5 — strong evidence for child maltreatment prevention, cost ~$4,500/family/year, requires weekly visits. The trade-off: NFP delivers the strongest per-family outcomes but serves the narrowest population; PAT serves the broadest population but with lower per-family effect sizes. Most family service organizations benefit from a tiered model: universal (PAT) for all enrolled families, with targeted escalation (HFA or NFP) for families with additional risk factors.

- **Developmental screening tool selection (ASQ-3 vs. PEDS vs. SWYC)**: ASQ-3 (Ages and Stages Questionnaire, 3rd edition): 21 age-specific questionnaires (1-66 months), 30 items per questionnaire covering communication, gross motor, fine motor, problem solving, personal-social — validated on diverse populations, sensitivity 86%, specificity 85%, parent-completed in 10-15 minutes, available in multiple languages. Use ASQ-3 for comprehensive developmental monitoring in center-based or home visiting programs where staff can assist parents with completion. PEDS (Parents' Evaluation of Developmental Status): 10 questions, 2-5 minutes to complete, sensitivity 74-79%, specificity 70-80% — faster screening for high-volume settings (pediatric primary care, early intervention intake). Use PEDS when screening volume is high and staff time is limited; follow up positive screens with ASQ-3 for domain-specific detail. SWYC (Survey of Well-being of Young Children): combines developmental milestones with autism screening and family risk factors — one integrated instrument for comprehensive well-child surveillance. Use SWYC when a single instrument covering development + behavior + family context is preferred over administering separate tools. The limitation: all screening tools have false positive and false negative rates — a positive screen requires a diagnostic evaluation (not just a referral); a negative screen in the presence of parent concern should still trigger further assessment per AAP guidelines.

- **CLASS vs. ERS for program quality assessment**: CLASS (Classroom Assessment Scoring System) measures teacher-child interaction quality across three domains (emotional support, classroom organization, instructional support) through 2-hour live observation cycles — it is process-quality focused and predicts child outcomes more strongly than structural measures. ERS (ECERS-R/ITERS-R/FCCERS-R) measures the physical environment, materials, schedule, and health/safety practices — it is structural-quality focused and most useful for licensing, accreditation, and environment improvement planning. Use CLASS for teacher coaching and professional development (specific interaction dimensions with behavioral anchors); use ERS for comprehensive environment assessment and QRIS compliance. The trade-off: CLASS requires certified observers (2-day training + reliability test) and provides richer feedback for teacher development; ERS can be conducted with less training and provides a broader facility-level picture. Combined use of CLASS + ERS provides the most complete quality assessment.

- **In-house vs. community-based service delivery**: In-house delivery (all services under one roof: parent education, developmental screening, early childhood education, family support) provides integrated care coordination and warm handoffs — no family is lost to referral leakage. But in-house requires broader staffing capability, higher fixed costs, and may duplicate existing community resources. Community-based partnership network (refer families to existing community providers: WIC, Early Intervention, mental health, housing) leverages existing infrastructure and avoids duplication but introduces referral leakage (30-50% of referrals never result in a completed appointment). Choose in-house for core services (parent education, screening); partner for specialized services (mental health, substance use treatment, pediatric medical care, housing assistance) with a care coordinator who tracks referral completion. The limitation: in-house models risk mission drift into areas without organizational expertise; partnership models risk fragmentation and families falling through service gaps.
